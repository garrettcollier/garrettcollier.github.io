Featured Keynote

You Can Do Everything Right and Still Lose

And that doesn’t mean you failed.

We often grow up believing that hard work, good decisions, and treating people well will eventually produce the outcome we deserve. But life never agreed to that bargain. A good decision can end badly. A reckless one can succeed. What, then, should we measure?

This keynote examines outcome bias, the difference between responsibility and control, and the limits of the scoreboards we use to judge our lives. Drawing on software development, public service, personal experience, and philosophical reflection, it offers a way to evaluate setbacks honestly without abandoning accountability, ambition, or care for other people.

The central idea: An outcome tells us what happened. It cannot, by itself, tell us whether the decision was sound or whether the effort was worthwhile.

A general outline

  1. The bargain life never signed. Why preparation and good intentions matter, even though they cannot guarantee success.
  2. Good decisions, bad outcomes. Everyday examples of outcome bias and the temptation to judge decisions using information available only afterward.
  3. Responsibility and control. Taking ownership of our choices without assuming responsibility for every variable that affects the result.
  4. A more useful scoreboard. Three questions: How did I prepare? Was the decision reasonable given what I knew? What did I do next?
  5. Choosing what comes next. Acknowledging loss, owning genuine mistakes, and continuing to learn, serve, and take worthwhile risks.

Purpose and audience takeaways

The purpose is to give people a more precise way to think about success and failure while preserving their responsibility to act. The talk leaves room for loss to hurt and for understanding to take time. Participants are invited to:

  • Distinguish the quality of a decision from the outcome that followed it.
  • Distinguish what can be changed, what can be influenced, and what remains outside our control, while taking responsibility for genuine mistakes.
  • Use preparation, decision, and response as a framework for reflection and future choices.
Intended audiences
Universities, leadership programs, civic organizations, professional groups, and general audiences.
Suggested format
A 25–40 minute keynote, with optional discussion. Length and emphasis can be discussed for the event.
Tone

Institutions · Technology · Public Service

The Systems We Inherit

Most of us work inside systems we did not design. This talk explores how institutional incentives, technical decisions, and ordinary habits shape what those systems produce, and how to improve them without pretending we can start from scratch.

Potential focus: Understanding the existing process, making tradeoffs visible, and connecting technical improvements with the people an institution serves.

Intended audiences: Public administrators, civic organizations, technology teams, and students.

Related research: USC MPA capstone

Preparedness · Leadership · Response

How Will You Respond?

Preparing for the Moment We Hope Never Comes.

Prevention matters, but it cannot promise complete control. This talk explores why preparedness depends on people, shared language, practiced relationships, and systems capable of responding when events depart from the plan.

Potential focus: The distinction between having a plan and being able to act together when it is needed.

Intended audiences: Education leaders, nonprofits, public-service organizations, and preparedness-focused events.
