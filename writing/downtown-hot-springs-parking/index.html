Downtown Parking in Hot Springs

A plain-language reading of the 159-page Walker study, where downtown parking stands today, and the costs and choices behind user fees, public subsidy, and a new garage.

Garrett Collier, MPA September 30, 2026 · Updated October 15, 2026 Estimated reading time: 14 minutes

Reading guide: This article summarizes Walker’s 2023 study and connects it with current policy and budget information. Historical observations, projected demand, budget figures, and illustrative construction costs describe different things; the distinctions matter.

Downtown parking is one of those local issues that seems simple until you actually look at the system.

A customer circles Central Avenue looking for a space near a shop. An employee needs somewhere to leave a car for an entire shift. A visitor enters the Exchange Street garage hoping to park once and spend the afternoon downtown. Nearby, spaces in a private lot may sit empty without being available to any of them.

All of those concerns can be true at the same time.

Each person sees a different part of the same system. That helps explain why downtown parking can be frustrating even when spaces are available elsewhere.

That is why the 2023 Walker Consultants parking study for Hot Springs is worth reading closely. It is also 159 pages long, which is one way of saying it is not exactly a beach read unless your idea of a beach read includes occupancy tables and parking-deck cost ranges.

But there is useful information in those pages: parking inventories, demand observations, community feedback, management recommendations, and alternatives for a new parking structure.

The purpose of this article is to make that resource easier to understand, connect its findings with today’s parking policy and budget, and explain the choices underneath the debate.

In plain English, the question is this:

How should Hot Springs make downtown parking work, who should pay for it, and what should the public receive in return?

Answering that requires looking at the spaces we already have, the people who use them, and the difference between maintaining a garage and financing another one.

Where Hot Springs Is Now

Paid parking is already part of downtown’s system. The city’s published parking instructions list enforcement from 8 a.m. to 6 p.m., Monday through Saturday, with two paid zones:

  • Priority Parking Zone: Central Avenue from Whittington Avenue to Olive Street, at $4 per hour with a four-hour maximum.
  • Secondary Parking Zone: locations including Fountain Street, Hill Wheatley Plaza, and paid portions of the Exchange Street Parking Deck, at $2 per hour with no time limit.

The garage’s upper levels, 3A and 3B, are designated for free parking. The city also identifies open parking zones without meters or time limits. Its current parking map and posted signs are important because the rules vary by location.

Payment options include kiosks, Park Smarter, ParkMobile, and text-to-pay. The posted payment zone codes are 50101 for priority parking and 50102 for secondary parking.

Those rules describe how the system operates. The Walker study helps explain why different parts of downtown may need different treatment.

What the Walker Study Found

Walker’s April 18, 2023 report has two main parts: an analysis of parking supply, demand, and community input, followed by an evaluation of parking alternatives. Its observations came from September 2022, including the Ladies in Leather rally weekend, with adjustments to estimate peak August conditions.

That timing matters. These findings describe the conditions Walker studied and projected. They provide a useful baseline, but they are not a measurement of today’s occupancy under today’s rates.

The report identified 3,294 spaces available for public use in its study area. Most were in lots and garages rather than along the street. Publicly usable also does not mean publicly owned: the off-street inventory included both public and private facilities.

At the time of observation, the approximately 179 metered on-street spaces generally charged 50 cents per hour. That historical rate should be read separately from the city’s current $4 and $2 zones.

Walker projected that about 39 percent of the overall study-area supply would be occupied under its peak-season scenario. Taken alone, that figure might suggest parking should be easy. Looking more closely changes the picture.

Demand was concentrated near Central Avenue and the Exchange Street garage. Walker projected Central Avenue’s on-street spaces could reach full occupancy and the garage could reach approximately 94 percent occupancy. The Central Area overall was projected at about 66 percent.

A driver looking for a convenient space in that core could therefore encounter a genuine shortage while parking elsewhere in the larger study area remained underused.

Walking distance, ownership restrictions, signs, accessibility, perceived safety, and knowledge of available spaces all affect whether parking is useful. An empty space several blocks away is less helpful if a visitor cannot find it, an employee cannot use it, or the walking route is difficult.

The study’s central lesson is that the location and usability of parking matter alongside the total number of spaces. More construction may eventually be warranted, but better management of existing capacity deserves attention first.

Customers and Employees Need Different Things

That distinction becomes especially clear when considering downtown workers.

Walker estimated roughly 500 full-time and 220 part-time employees associated with Central Avenue businesses. After accounting for designated parking, it estimated about 480 employees might need to find parking on a typical day.

A customer may need a space for an hour. An employee may need one for an entire shift. Both have legitimate needs, but those uses compete when they depend on the same limited curb spaces.

Walker recommended addressing employee parking before implementing on-street paid parking. The practical reason is straightforward: encouraging employees to leave the most desirable customer spaces only works if they have realistic alternatives.

This does not make employees the villain. Downtown workers need affordable, safe, predictable places to park during actual work hours. Residents also need dependable arrangements.

A parking plan that treats employees as an afterthought is not a plan. It is a complaint with meters attached.

This is where parking policy becomes more than a rate schedule. It has to connect short visits, longer stays, employee parking, resident needs, loading, and accessibility into a system people can understand.

Making Better Use of What We Already Have

Walker’s “No Build Option” follows directly from that diagnosis. It describes actions the city could take to improve parking without immediately constructing a new garage.

The recommendations include shared-parking agreements, potential acquisition of private lots, dedicated employee areas, better maps and signs, updated payment systems, adjustments to enforcement and parking rules, and clearer public communication.

Shared parking is particularly relevant where different users need the same spaces at different times. The report identified possibilities involving the Old Regions Bank deck, the Hotel Hot Springs garage, the U.S. Forestry lot, county overflow parking, and other locations.

These were opportunities for exploration, not a declaration that the spaces were available for unrestricted public use. Agreements would have to address compensation, liability, access, enforcement, and the owners’ own needs. Hotel peaks, church services, funerals, and special events can change availability.

The existing garage also needs attention. Walker identified opportunities to improve circulation, garage and elevator maintenance, and wayfinding technology. It described the difficulty of reaching a full upper level with limited room to turn around.

These details matter because a new garage would not automatically correct problems in the one already serving downtown. Maintaining existing assets and making available spaces easier to use are part of the same responsibility.

In plain terms: do the obvious operational work before making the expensive capital bet.

Paid parking can support that work. Walker discussed it as a way to encourage turnover, generate revenue, and direct longer stays toward less constrained locations. It also recommended visible improvements, clear communication, and some free parking for people willing and able to park farther away.

The next question is what those services cost, and how the city should divide that cost between users and the broader public.

What the Parking Budget Actually Shows

The 2026 city budget, on page 28, projects approximately $653,000 in parking-fee revenue and lists the following parking-fund expenses:

The budget describes two full-time employees and says most services and supplies relate to deck maintenance and parking-management software and equipment. Depreciation accounts for asset wear over time; it is not a current cash payment.

Personnel and services total $566,711, approximately $86,289 below projected fee revenue. Including depreciation puts expenses approximately $154,597 above that revenue. Neither comparison alone establishes available cash for construction, debt payments, or reserves.

These are projections for the parking system, not garage-only costs or actual year-end results.

That distinction is essential to a fair discussion of subsidy. The garage’s annual funding requirement needs its own accounting.

What Keeping the Garage Free Would Mean

A garage can be free to the driver while still carrying substantial public costs.

The elevator repair bill does not disappear because nobody paid to park.

Lighting, cleaning, inspections, insurance, elevator service, repairs, and long-term structural maintenance continue whether a payment is collected at the entrance or not. A facility also needs a plan for larger repairs as it ages.

There is a legitimate argument for public support. Convenient parking can serve downtown workers, residents, businesses, and visitors. Free or discounted access might be an intentional way to support those users.

There is also a legitimate fee-for-service argument: people who use a maintained facility can reasonably contribute toward providing it. A user fee and a public contribution can exist together, with each covering a defined share.

Other communities illustrate that range of choices. Fayetteville’s Spring Street and West Avenue decks combine paid parking with two free daytime hours. The Branson Convention Center garage also charges users. Those examples show that charging for a garage is an established approach, while the details of access and pricing remain local decisions.

To evaluate making the Exchange Street garage entirely free, residents need a facility-specific estimate. It should identify recurring operating costs, upcoming major repairs, any outstanding debt obligations, garage receipts that would disappear, and collection expenses that could be avoided.

The city should then explain how much support would come from retained parking revenue elsewhere, other dedicated receipts, reserves, or tax-supported funds. Those are different funding choices. The amount of garage revenue forgone is not automatically the amount of additional tax funding required.

Long-term maintenance also belongs in the discussion, with care taken to avoid counting both depreciation and the same replacement expense as separate cash obligations.

The budget summary above does not isolate the full cost of subsidizing the garage. Calling the entire parking-fund budget the cost of free garage parking would overstate what those figures establish.

A complete proposal should show the annual contribution, its funding source, the longer-term commitment, and the expected benefit. Where those funds could legally support other priorities, residents should also understand the alternatives: maintenance elsewhere, sidewalks, parks, public safety, or other community needs.

The policy choice is whether the benefits of free access justify that contribution compared with other eligible uses of the money. That deserves an open discussion with the numbers attached.

What Walker Considered for a New Garage

Maintaining the existing garage is one decision. Adding another structure creates a separate construction and financing obligation.

Walker’s structured-parking analysis considered a 500-space concept and evaluated five site alternatives. Its weighted ranking provides useful background for understanding the options examined in 2023.

A ranking is a planning aid. Property availability, agreements, walking routes, engineering, and present-day conditions would still need review before selecting a site.

Walker used a planning range of $25,000 to $50,000 per structured space. Applied to 500 spaces, that produces $12.5 million to $25 million. Those are figures from the 2023 report, not a current project estimate.

Newer evidence helps put that dated range in context. WGI’s 2026 cost outlook reports a national median construction cost of $33,300 per space. A UCLA report published in February 2026, using 2025 construction data from 17 cities, reports an above-ground average of approximately $52,000 per space.

The newer benchmarks use different datasets and methods; they are not interchangeable bids. UCLA’s approximately $73,000 underground average describes a different type of construction and should not be used as the expected price of an above-ground garage.

For an illustrative scenario of $50,000 to $75,000 per space, the same 500-space concept would cost $25 million to $37.5 million. That is a sensitivity range, not a verified Hot Springs estimate or a claim that all garages now cost that amount.

Construction cost also differs from a complete project budget. Land, demolition, site preparation, utilities, design, permitting, contingencies, and financing need to be identified, with any costs already included in a benchmark accounted for. A current local estimate should state its scope and pricing date.

The scale of the investment explains Walker’s caution about expecting either an individual structure or the parking enterprise to pay for a new garage under the conditions it studied. That warning remains useful background, while a present-day financing decision requires updated evidence.

The Fiscal Concern Is Legitimate

A new garage may provide benefits beyond the fees it collects. It could support longer visits, improve access, serve future development, or reduce pressure in particular locations. Those benefits should be evaluated alongside construction, operation, and long-term maintenance.

If parking revenue is proposed as the repayment source, the relevant figure is reliable revenue remaining after the obligations that must be paid first. Gross collections alone do not answer the question.

Borrowing spreads the construction payment over time and adds financing costs. A workable plan needs to show annual debt payments, operating expenses, maintenance reserves, revenue assumptions, and what happens if collections fall short.

If a new garage were entirely free to use, its own parking charges would contribute nothing toward those obligations. The plan would need to identify what other revenue would cover them and what public contribution would remain necessary.

Charging users would change that calculation, but it would not guarantee that the facility could finance itself. The contribution from fees depends on use, rates, collection costs, and the facility’s other expenses.

This is consistent with the approach I take in my writing on economic development and infrastructure financing: identify the need, explain the financial obligation, and ask whether the public benefit justifies the public cost.

There may be a sound reason for a subsidy. Residents should be able to see its amount, duration, funding source, and expected result before being asked to support it.

Accountability Should Follow the Money

The Walker report offers a starting point for that conversation. Current operating information should show what has changed since the study and whether today’s policies are accomplishing their goals.

A useful public report would connect:

  • Access: occupancy, length of stay, and turnover in the core, alongside use of garages and other lots.
  • Practical alternatives: employee and resident arrangements, available free spaces, shared-parking agreements, signs, and walking routes.
  • Service: garage condition, elevator reliability, lighting, repairs, and planned maintenance.
  • Money: collections by location, operating and collection costs, capital needs, and any public contributions.
  • Future commitments: a defined project and financing plan if additional construction remains a goal.

Success should be measured against the purpose of each charge. Central Avenue turnover, a reliable garage, and a construction reserve can all be legitimate objectives, but each needs an explanation and a way to assess progress.

That also creates a fair basis for changing the program. Rates, hours, discounts, and the paid-parking footprint should be reviewed as evidence develops.

Where I Land

I support paid parking on Central Avenue because those spaces should turn over for customers and short visits. The goal should be dependable access to downtown businesses and destinations. Supporting that purpose does not mean every current rate or restriction is beyond review.

I also support reasonable fees to use the Exchange Street garage when those fees serve a defined plan for operating, maintaining, and improving the facility. The fee-for-service principle makes sense to me here: users can contribute toward a public facility that costs money to provide.

Paying to use a garage is familiar to me from visiting Little Rock, Northwest Arkansas, and places such as Branson. What I expect in return is a facility that is maintained, easy to use, and worth the fee.

That support comes with expectations. Workers and residents need practical alternatives. The garage should be maintained. Revenue and spending should be understandable. Any tax-supported contribution should have a stated amount, a public purpose, and regular review.

Keeping a garage free may be a defensible choice, but I would want to know what the public is committing each year and why that commitment is preferable to other eligible uses of the money. The same standard applies even more strongly to financing a new garage.

I support paid parking on Central Avenue and in the garage when the city can show the purpose, the cost, and the result. If the broader program cannot meet that standard, Hot Springs should reevaluate it and scale paid parking back primarily to Central Avenue to maintain turnover.

The 159-page Walker study helps residents understand the options. The city’s responsibility now is to connect those options to current evidence and a clear public plan.

Hot Springs should have that conversation in public, with the math on the table.

Residents should be able to understand what parking fees pay for, what any subsidy buys, and how those decisions make downtown work better.
