License Plate Readers in Hot Springs

Flock cameras, public safety, privacy, private operators, and the questions we should ask before a useful tool becomes an unexamined system.

Garrett Collier, MPA September 29, 2026 Estimated reading time: 12 minutes

Publication note: This op-ed is part of my permanent writing archive at garrettcollier.net. It is also the kind of essay I expect to share through Substack so readers can subscribe, discuss, and follow future pieces while the website remains the source of record.

A friend recently described what a lot of people feel when local public issues show up on social media: a sea of emotion, scattered facts, and very little calm explanation.

That is exactly the space where I think longer-form local writing can be useful.

Not because one essay should be treated as the final word. It should not.

But because some issues deserve more than a hot take, a screenshot, or a slogan.

License plate readers are one of those issues.

In Hot Springs, the conversation often gets shortened into one word: Flock.

That word matters, because Flock Safety is a major vendor in the license-plate-reader space. But the larger issue is broader than one company. It is about automated license plate readers, public-safety investigations, location data, private camera operators, retention rules, data sharing, oversight, and public trust.

So the useful question is not simply: Are Flock cameras good or bad?

The better question is: What rules should apply when public or private institutions collect searchable records of vehicles moving through our community?

What I Learned from HSPD

I recently spoke with Chief Chapmond to better understand what is currently under the Hot Springs Police Department's purview.

According to Chief Chapmond, HSPD has 15 license plate reader cameras under its purview. Of those 15, five are Flock cameras.

He also told me that none of the 15 use facial-recognition technology.

That last point is important because facial recognition often enters the public conversation around surveillance technology, sometimes accurately and sometimes not. Based on what Chief Chapmond told me, the local HSPD issue is not facial recognition.

It is license plate recognition.

That distinction should lower the temperature of the debate without ending the debate. A technology can avoid facial recognition and still raise serious questions about vehicle-location data, retention, access, sharing, and oversight.

What License Plate Readers Do

An automated license plate reader is a camera system that captures images of vehicles and uses software to identify license plate numbers. Depending on the system and configuration, records may include the plate image, vehicle characteristics, date and time, and the camera location.

Flock's own trust materials describe its ALPR system as collecting license plate images, vehicle characteristics, date and time stamps, and camera-location identifiers. Flock also states that its ALPR system does not collect biometric data, facial-recognition data, or driver information.

Those distinctions matter.

A license plate reader is not the same thing as a camera identifying a person by face. But it can still create a record showing that a vehicle associated with a person was in a particular place at a particular time.

That can be useful for law enforcement.

It can also be sensitive.

In a single instance, a plate read may seem mundane. Over time, across enough cameras, searchable vehicle-location records can reveal patterns: work routines, medical visits, church attendance, school pickup routes, political events, private meetings, and the ordinary movements of everyday life.

That is why the governance question matters.

Why Police Use Them

There are legitimate public-safety reasons law enforcement agencies use license plate readers.

They can help identify stolen vehicles, wanted vehicles, vehicles connected to investigations, missing-person cases, Amber Alerts, and other time-sensitive public-safety concerns. In a city with tourism, regional traffic, parks, schools, commercial corridors, and residential neighborhoods, vehicle-related investigations are not unusual.

If a stolen car enters Hot Springs, or a vehicle connected to a serious crime passes through a camera location, police may receive information faster than they otherwise would.

That can matter.

I want to be clear about that. I support law enforcement having effective tools to solve serious crimes, find dangerous suspects, recover stolen vehicles, and protect the public.

That support is part of why the policy questions matter. Clear rules do not weaken legitimate law enforcement work. They strengthen public trust, protect residents from misuse, and protect officers and departments from confusion, rumor, and avoidable controversy.

But usefulness is not the same thing as unlimited justification. Many government tools are useful. The question is whether they are used under clear rules, with appropriate limits, and with enough transparency for the public to understand the system being built around them.

What Arkansas Law Allows

Arkansas law does not ban license plate readers.

The Arkansas Automatic License Plate Reader System Act allows use of ALPR systems by law enforcement agencies. It also allows use for parking enforcement, for controlling access to secured areas, and for specified highway-police commercial-vehicle functions.

That is a major point for Hot Springs residents to understand.

This is not only a police issue.

Private businesses, parking operators, institutions, and other non-police entities may also appear in the broader license-plate-reader ecosystem. Those systems may not all be controlled by the same public policies, public-records expectations, or public oversight processes that apply to police agencies.

The Arkansas statute also matters because it limits how captured plate data may be used, shared, preserved, and destroyed. Arkansas law generally requires captured plate data to be destroyed no later than 150 days after collection unless the data is being preserved for a law-enforcement purpose or other authorized purpose under the statute.

That legal ceiling is important. But local policy can still matter inside that ceiling: shorter retention periods, stricter access controls, written search requirements, audit practices, and transparency reports can all affect how the tool actually works in practice.

The Public Maps Are Helpful, But Not Official

One reason this issue is difficult for ordinary residents is that there is not always an easy official inventory showing every license plate reader in a community, who operates it, and what rules apply.

Independent public maps try to fill that gap.

Finding Flock, an independent project built from OpenStreetMap volunteer reports, currently lists 31 documented automated license plate reader locations in Hot Springs. Its Hot Springs page identifies 30 of those as Flock Safety units, one as a Motorola Solutions unit, and also records at least one non-government operator, Lowe's.

That does not mean HSPD operates 31 Flock cameras. It does not.

It also does not mean every mapped camera is necessarily active, current, or officially verified. Finding Flock itself says these locations are evidence of where cameras have been seen, not a complete or official inventory. Another public map, DeFlock, is also built around public reporting and mapping rather than an official local-government inventory.

This is exactly the point.

Public volunteer maps are useful leads. They help residents see that license plate readers are not limited to one department or one public agency. But they should not have to serve as the only practical way for residents to understand the technology around them.

If cameras are operating in public or quasi-public spaces, residents should be able to understand who operates them, what they collect, how long data is retained, who can access it, and who data is shared with.

Private Operators Matter Too

Most privacy conversations focus on government, and for good reason. Government has coercive power. Government can investigate, detain, charge, fine, prosecute, regulate, and enforce.

But private camera networks still matter.

If a business, shopping center, apartment complex, school, church, neighborhood association, or other institution uses license plate readers, the public questions do not disappear. They change form.

Residents may reasonably ask:

  • Who owns or operates the camera?
  • What data is collected?
  • How long is it retained?
  • Who can search it?
  • Is it shared with law enforcement automatically, by request, by policy, or not at all?
  • Is the public notified that the system exists?
  • Can misuse be audited?
  • Who is accountable if data is used improperly?

Those are not anti-police questions. They are governance questions.

They are also questions we should ask of private institutions, not only public agencies.

A community can support law enforcement, respect private property, and still expect clear rules for technology that records vehicle movements at scale.

What HSPD Should Be Able to Answer Publicly

Based on what I know right now, I am not writing this as an accusation against HSPD.

Chief Chapmond answered basic questions directly: 15 LPR cameras under HSPD purview, five Flock cameras, and no facial recognition among those 15.

That is useful information.

Now the public conversation should move from basic inventory to policy and accountability.

Here are the questions I think HSPD should be able to answer in a clear public format:

  • Where is the written LPR or ALPR policy?
  • What crimes or public-safety purposes justify a search?
  • Who is authorized to search the system?
  • Is a case number, incident number, or written reason required before a search?
  • How long is HSPD-retained data kept?
  • Does HSPD use a shorter retention period than the maximum Arkansas law allows?
  • Are searches logged?
  • Who reviews search logs?
  • What happens if someone searches the system without a legitimate purpose?
  • What agencies, if any, can access HSPD plate data?
  • What outside databases, if any, can HSPD search through its systems?
  • How often does the technology produce investigative leads?
  • How often are those leads useful?
  • How often are they wrong?
  • What does the city pay for the systems, and when do contracts renew?

Some of these answers may already exist in internal policy, contracts, logs, procurement documents, or vendor dashboards.

The public value comes from making the answers easy to find and easy to understand.

I plan to follow up with Chief Chapmond and HSPD on these questions. If HSPD provides additional policy details, retention information, or public documentation, I will either update this piece or publish a follow-up so residents can evaluate the answers for themselves.

Transparency Should Not Require Suspicion

One of the worst habits in public life is treating transparency as something only critics ask for.

That is backwards.

Transparency protects the public, but it also protects good public servants. Clear rules make it easier for officers to use tools properly, easier for supervisors to identify misuse, easier for city leaders to evaluate value, and easier for residents to distinguish legitimate public-safety technology from rumor or fear.

If the policy is strong, publish it.

If searches are logged, explain how logs are reviewed.

If the system is limited to legitimate public-safety purposes, say what those purposes are.

If data is not shared with certain agencies, say so.

If data can be shared, explain with whom and under what conditions.

If the cameras do not use facial recognition, repeat that clearly.

Residents should not have to piece together public policy from vendor websites, volunteer maps, social media arguments, and hallway conversations.

What Good Policy Could Look Like

A reasonable license plate reader policy does not have to begin with banning the technology.

It should begin with purpose.

Why does Hot Springs use LPR technology? What specific problems is it meant to help solve? How will we know whether it is working?

Then it should define limits.

Searches should require a legitimate public-safety purpose. Access should be limited to authorized users. Every search should be logged. Supervisors should review usage. Misuse should have consequences. Retention should be as short as practical, not merely as long as legally allowed. Data sharing should be explicit, narrow, and publicly disclosed.

The city should also consider a simple public transparency page that includes:

  • Number of LPR cameras under city or police control.
  • Vendors used.
  • Contracts and renewal dates.
  • Written policy.
  • Retention period.
  • Authorized uses.
  • External sharing rules.
  • Annual usage summary.
  • Contact information for questions.

This does not require publishing sensitive investigative details or real-time camera locations if that would compromise legitimate public-safety operations.

It does require explaining the system at a level ordinary residents can understand.

Where I Land

I do not think Hot Springs should treat license plate readers as automatically sinister.

I also do not think we should treat them as automatically harmless.

The honest position is somewhere more serious than either reflex.

License plate readers can be a legitimate public-safety tool. They can help police locate stolen vehicles, respond to serious investigations, and identify vehicles connected to real public-safety needs.

But they also create searchable records of vehicle movement. That deserves rules, oversight, and public explanation.

Based on my conversation with Chief Chapmond, the local HSPD system should not be discussed as facial recognition. That is not what he described. The better public conversation is about vehicle-location data: collection, retention, access, sharing, auditing, private operators, and accountability.

My view is that Hot Springs can use license plate reader technology responsibly only if the public can understand the system clearly enough to evaluate it.

That means HSPD should publish or clearly summarize its policy, retention period, access controls, sharing rules, audit practices, and procurement information.

It also means private institutions using similar technology should be willing to answer basic questions about what they collect, how long they keep it, and who can access it.

The goal should not be panic.

The goal should be public trust.

And public trust is built by answering fair questions before people are forced to assume the answers.
