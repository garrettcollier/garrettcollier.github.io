Public Administration Op-Ed

Mayors, City Managers, and the Government We Actually Have

Local government is not just about who has the title. It is about who sets policy, who runs operations, who answers to voters, and whether residents can tell the difference.

Garrett Collier, MPA October 5, 2026 Estimated reading time: 12 minutes

Plain-English version: A mayor-council system makes the mayor a central executive figure. A council-manager system gives elected officials policy authority while a professional manager runs day-to-day administration. The question is not which title sounds stronger. The question is which structure creates better accountability for the kind of city Hot Springs is.

People often talk about city government as if every city works the same way.

It does not.

In one city, the mayor may function like a local chief executive. In another, the mayor may preside over meetings, represent the city publicly, and vote as one member of a governing board. In another, elected officials may hire a professional city manager to run operations while they set policy.

Those differences matter.

They shape who controls departments, who prepares budgets, who hires and fires staff, who negotiates with outside entities, who gets blamed when services fail, and who residents expect to fix problems.

So when people say a city needs a "strong mayor," a "city manager," or a "better board," the first useful question is simple:

What problem are we trying to solve?

The Basic Models

American cities generally operate under a handful of common local-government forms. The names vary by state, charter, and statute, but the basic tension is usually the same: elected political leadership versus professional administrative management.

In a mayor-council system, the mayor is usually more directly tied to executive administration. The mayor may supervise departments, appoint officials, prepare budgets, and act as the public face of city action. Some mayor-council systems are stronger than others, but the public expectation is usually clear: the mayor is the central elected executive.

In a council-manager system, voters elect a council or board to set policy. That elected body hires a professional manager to run the city's day-to-day operations. The manager does not replace democracy. The manager works under elected officials who can hire, evaluate, direct, or remove the manager.

That distinction is important because the council-manager model is sometimes misunderstood as less democratic.

It is not less democratic if elected officials are doing their job.

It is a different division of labor. Voters choose policymakers. Policymakers hire an administrator. The administrator runs the machinery of government under public policy direction.

What Arkansas Allows

Arkansas cities do not all operate under one structure.

Arkansas municipal law has long recognized different forms of city government, including mayor-council government, city-manager government, city-administrator government, and board-of-directors structures. The Arkansas Municipal League's municipal-official manual explains these forms for city officials and describes the mayor-council form as the most common arrangement for Arkansas cities.

That matters because Arkansas is not choosing between one normal model and one strange model. Arkansas already contains multiple municipal structures.

A city-manager or board-of-directors form is not imported from nowhere. It is part of the legal toolkit available to Arkansas municipalities.

What Arkansas Cities Actually Use

The Arkansas breakdown is still heavily tilted toward mayor-council government.

The Arkansas Municipal League's 2025 municipal directory lists five Arkansas cities as city-manager cities: Arkadelphia, Hope, Hot Springs, Little Rock, and Texarkana. It lists three as administrator cities: Barling, Fort Smith, and Siloam Springs. Then it says all other Arkansas cities are mayor-council cities.

That is a useful fact, but it should be read carefully.

It means Hot Springs is in a smaller category inside Arkansas. It does not mean Hot Springs is doing something legally unusual or inherently wrong. It means Hot Springs has chosen a form that places more administrative responsibility in a professional manager while leaving policy authority with elected officials.

Hot Springs Has a City Manager System

Hot Springs does not operate as a strong-mayor city.

Hot Springs uses a city-manager form of government. Residents elect a Board of Directors. The Board sets policy, passes ordinances, adopts budgets, and appoints the city manager. The city manager runs the administrative side of city government.

The mayor matters, but not in the same way a strong mayor would.

In this structure, the mayor is part of the elected governing body and has important public, procedural, and leadership responsibilities. But the mayor is not supposed to personally run every city department. That responsibility belongs to the city manager, subject to the policy direction and accountability of the elected Board.

That is not a small job.

The City of Hot Springs says the city manager is responsible for managing all city departments and funds, hiring and supervising all city employees, and implementing policy decisions of the Board of Directors. The city's Human Resources page describes a workforce of more than 600 full- and part-time employees across 21 departments and 25 departmental divisions.

In practical terms, that means the city manager is the chief administrative officer over the organization residents experience every day: police, fire, utilities, public works, planning and development, parks, transit, solid waste, engineering, finance, information systems, human resources, the airport, animal services, parking facilities, and the department heads who lead that work.

That is why the distinction between policy and administration is not academic. It determines who is responsible for setting direction and who is responsible for running a large public-service organization.

This is where public confusion can start.

If residents expect the mayor to act like a local president, but the legal structure gives operational authority to a city manager, frustration can be aimed at the wrong office. And if elected board members treat the city manager as a shield rather than a person they are responsible for directing and evaluating, accountability can become too blurry.

What Surrounding States Show

The broader regional picture is mixed. Nearby states do not all use the same vocabulary, and they do not all count municipal forms in exactly the same way. But the pattern is clear enough: mayor-led systems remain common, while council-manager and administrator models are also normal tools of local government.

That table should make us careful about easy slogans.

Popularity tells us what is familiar. It does not tell us what is best for a particular city.

Some communities want a highly visible elected executive. Others want professional administration under elected policy oversight. Others use hybrid arrangements shaped by state law, local history, city size, and political culture. The useful question is not which model sounds strongest. The useful question is which model gives the public competent administration, clear policy leadership, and accountability residents can actually understand.

The Tradeoff

The mayor-council model has real strengths.

It can create a clearer line of democratic responsibility. If the mayor has executive authority, voters know who to praise or blame. A strong mayor can set a public agenda, negotiate visibly, and give residents a recognizable point of leadership.

But the model also has risks.

City administration is technical. Budgets, utilities, personnel rules, procurement, infrastructure, land use, emergency services, finance, grants, bonds, audits, technology, and compliance are not campaign slogans. They are systems. A city can suffer if administration becomes too politicized, too personal, or too dependent on one elected official's managerial ability.

The council-manager model has a different strength.

It is designed to professionalize administration while keeping policy authority with elected representatives. In theory, this lets the city hire someone with administrative expertise to run complex operations while elected officials focus on public priorities, budgets, ordinances, and accountability.

But that model has risks too.

If the elected board is passive, the manager can become too dominant. If the board micromanages staff, the manager model breaks down from the other direction. If residents do not understand who is responsible for what, everyone can point somewhere else when accountability is needed.

Clear tradeoffs between common municipal forms
Model What it does well Where it can fail
Mayor-council Creates a visible elected executive, a clearer public face for citywide leadership, and a more direct line between voters and executive action. Can politicize administration, concentrate too much operational authority in one elected office, and depend heavily on one person's management skill.
Council-manager Separates policy from administration, professionalizes daily operations, and gives elected officials a manager they can hire, evaluate, direct, and replace. Can blur accountability if elected officials become passive, if the manager becomes too dominant, or if residents cannot tell who is responsible for what.

No structure automatically creates good government.

Structure only creates channels. People still have to use them well.

The Accountability Question

The real test is not whether a city has a mayor, a manager, a board, or a council.

The real test is whether residents can answer these questions:

  • Who sets policy?
  • Who runs daily operations?
  • Who hires and evaluates the chief administrator?
  • Who controls the budget?
  • Who can change direction when something is not working?
  • Who is publicly accountable to voters?

In a city-manager system, elected officials cannot fairly be blamed for every operational mistake made by every employee every day.

But they are responsible for policy direction, budget priorities, public oversight, and the performance of the manager they appoint.

That is the center of the model.

Professional administration should not become political insulation.

What Good City-Manager Government Requires

A city-manager system works best when the elected board is serious about governance.

That means board members should not try to personally manage departments. It also means they should not disappear into ceremonial approval.

Good board governance includes clear goals, public priorities, budget discipline, performance measures, manager evaluation, transparency, and the courage to ask hard questions in public.

The city manager should have enough administrative space to run the city professionally.

The elected board should have enough backbone to make sure professional administration is serving the public's priorities.

That balance is not always easy. But it is the point of the system.

Where I Land

I do not think Hot Springs needs to pretend it is a strong-mayor city.

Hot Springs is a complex service organization. It runs utilities, public safety services, parks, planning, permitting, public works, finance, tourism-related infrastructure, major capital projects, and long-term community systems. Many of those services also stretch beyond the city limits and into the county, which adds another layer of operational and political complexity. That kind of work benefits from professional administration.

So my starting point is that the city-manager model makes sense for Hot Springs.

But the model only works if elected officials embrace their side of the bargain.

A Board of Directors should not act like seven part-time department heads. It also should not act like a ceremonial audience for staff recommendations. The Board's job is to set priorities, ask serious questions, approve budgets, evaluate results, and make sure the city manager is advancing the public's goals.

The mayor still matters in that structure. A mayor can help set tone, organize public conversation, represent the city, build coalitions, explain decisions, and help residents understand what government is doing. But that role should complement the city-manager form, not confuse it.

My view is that Hot Springs does not need a louder title. It needs clearer accountability.

If residents want stronger leadership, the answer is not automatically to change the form of government. It may be to expect more from the elected officials already responsible for policy, oversight, and public direction.

We should also be wary of changing a form of government solely because another model is more popular, more familiar, or easier to explain. Popularity is not the same thing as fit. A government structure should be judged by whether it matches the city's actual responsibilities and gives residents a clear way to hold decision-makers accountable.

Good city-manager government is not government by staff.

It is elected leadership plus professional administration, with the line between them clear enough for the public to see.
